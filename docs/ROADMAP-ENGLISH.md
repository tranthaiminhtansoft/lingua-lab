## English learning scope

Build a broad **English learning path** for a Vietnamese-speaking learner who wants to communicate confidently across everyday, social, travel, study, and work situations. Use Vietnamese for explanations and translations, and English for lesson titles where useful, examples, and practice prompts. Teach in small steps: understand a model, notice the pattern, then use it in a realistic exchange. Include DevOps as a recurring professional context alongside general English, rather than making it the sole subject. Grow from beginner foundations toward independent intermediate communication.

### Learning path

| Order | Lesson group | Initial scope |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sentence foundations | Basic word order; subject, object, and possessive pronouns; *be* in statements, negatives, and questions; singular/plural nouns; *a/an/the*. Draw examples from personal introductions, places, objects, and work. |
| 2 | Introductions and everyday conversation | Introduce yourself and others; ask simple questions; talk about family, interests, routines, and plans; use polite expressions and clarification phrases. |
| 3 | Everyday actions and time | Present simple and present continuous; common verbs; questions; frequency, weekdays, dates, clock time, minutes, and seconds. Contrast habits with actions happening now. |
| 4 | Describe and compare | Adjectives, *have/has*, this/that, *there is/are*, and basic comparatives. Describe people, places, possessions, services, and options. |
| 5 | Practical situations | Food, shopping, directions, transport, travel, appointments, money/prices, and common service interactions. Practice asking for help, quantities, and handling simple misunderstandings. |
| 6 | Past experiences and future plans | Past simple for completed events; basic future forms for plans and predictions; talk about trips, study, personal goals, and planned work. |
| 7 | Work and professional communication | Meetings, status updates, requests, email/chat tone, explaining tasks, giving feedback, turn-taking, and collaborating. Include polite agreement/disagreement and DevOps scenarios such as deployments, monitoring, incidents, troubleshooting, and handoffs. |
| 8 | Explain, discuss, and present ideas | Organize a longer answer, compare choices, describe a process or trend, state an opinion with support, and summarize a recommendation. Use light IELTS-style prompts while keeping practice conversational and relevant to real life. |
| 9 | Build independent communication | Reading short practical texts; listening for main points and details; writing messages and short summaries; speaking with connected sentences and follow-up questions. Include spelling names/email addresses, numbers and units, and simple self-checks against stage outcomes. Recycle grammar and vocabulary across topics. |

Build grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, listening, speaking, reading, and writing together rather than treating grammar as the whole course. Begin with a compact grammar foundation, then apply each structure in useful situations. Each grammar pattern should show a formula, examples, a negative or question form where relevant, and a short exchange. Revisit key language in new contexts so learners can transfer it beyond the lesson where it was introduced. Explain specialist vocabulary briefly and pair it with plain-English alternatives.

### Suggested course order

The nine learning-path groups above describe **what** learners study; these six stages describe **when** to introduce it. A stage may draw examples from several topic groups. Use short stages with a clear communication outcome. Each stage reuses earlier language, and learners can review or skip ahead rather than being locked into a rigid course.

| Stage | Main focus | Learner can… |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Make basic sentences | *Be*, subject/object/possessive pronouns, nouns, *a/an/the*, basic negatives, yes/no and simple WH questions; greetings and introductions. | Introduce themselves and ask or answer simple personal questions. |
| 2. Talk about routines and needs | Present simple, *do/does*, WH questions, *can* for ability and basic requests, *want/need/have to*, frequency and time expressions; *at/on/in* for clock times, days, dates, and longer periods. | Talk about habits, ability, preferences, needs, responsibilities, and schedules. |
| 3. Talk about now and describe things | Present continuous; adjectives; *have/has*; *there is/are*; count/non-count nouns and simple quantities; basic comparisons; *in/on/at* and common position prepositions; polite *could you…?* requests and conversation extenders. | Describe a current situation, person, place, or service; say how much/how many; and keep a short conversation going. |
| 4. Tell what happened and make plans | Past simple with high-frequency irregular verbs; common future forms; time clauses with *when/while/before/after/as soon as*; movement prepositions; *because/so*; practical vocabulary for daily life, travel, study, and work. | Explain a past event, describe overlapping or ordered events, give a reason, and discuss a plan or next step. |
| 5. Connect ideas and ask indirectly | Present perfect; *since/for*; *if/whether*; comparison and paired conjunctions; relative clauses; contrast and purpose links. | Describe experience or duration, compare options, ask tactfully, and give a connected explanation. |
| 6. Choose a context or challenge | Passive, less common conjunctions, formal register, detailed work/DevOps scenarios, longer discussion, light IELTS-style speaking/writing tasks. | Explain a process or incident, discuss exceptions and recommendations, and adapt language to audience and purpose. |

Move a topic earlier when the learner’s goal needs it. For example, teach passive voice in Stage 3–4 for an operations learner who needs to report incidents; keep it in Stage 6 for a general beginner. Introduce *not only…but also* after simpler comparison and joining patterns, since it is useful for emphasis but less necessary for basic conversation.


### Priority and when to teach each structure

Prioritize by how often a learner needs the structure and what it lets them do. Introduce a structure when a realistic task calls for it, then revisit it in later topics; do not give every grammar item equal lesson time.

Base lesson order and examples on **real usage as well as teaching sequence**. When preparing lesson content, check spoken English separately from formal writing, prefer frequent and broadly useful phrases for early lessons, and label register where a form is formal, casual, or work-specific. Use CEFR level information to pace vocabulary and grammar, and use learner-error evidence to decide which contrasts need extra practice. Corpus frequency is evidence for prioritization, not a rule that makes less frequent forms unimportant; a learner’s goals can move a useful structure earlier.

| Priority | Teach | When and why |
|---|---|---|
| **Core — early** | *be* and action-verb sentence patterns; negatives; yes/no and WH questions; present simple; present continuous; past simple with common irregular verbs; basic future plans; *can* for ability and common requests; *want / need / have to*; *at/on/in* for common time expressions; common time/frequency adverbs; *because/so*; basic comparisons; greetings, thanks, apologies, polite requests, and follow-up questions. | These let learners introduce themselves, ask for information, describe routines and current actions, report a simple past event or plan, express ability, preference, or obligation, react politely, and keep a conversation going. Practice them from the first lessons and recycle them often. |
| **Useful — after foundations** | *might/may* for possibility; *should* for advice/expectation; present perfect; *before/after* and *since/for*; *if* conditions; extended comparisons; *although/even though/though* and *despite/in spite of*; *both…and / either…or / neither…nor*; indirect questions with *if/whether*; common adverbials; purpose and sequence links; *who/which/that*; polite suggestions. | Add these when learners can already make basic sentences and the task needs uncertainty, advice, experience, duration, alternatives, indirectness, contrast, or more connected speech. Start with common spoken forms and use examples to show distinctions. |
| **Situational — introduce on demand** | Passive voice; *unless*; *not only…but also*; *therefore*; *in order to*; *would you mind…?*; *used to*; *must* for strict rules or deduction; *may I…?* for formal permission; more formal contrast and result connectors. | Teach these when learners need to explain a process or incident, write a more formal message, emphasize a point, describe an exception or past habit, state a strict rule or evidence-based conclusion, or make a carefully softened request. They are useful, but should not delay basic conversation. |

These tiers are flexible: a learner working in operations may need passive voice and incident language earlier than someone focused on travel, while a learner preparing for an exam may need comparison and longer-answer practice sooner. Keep the shared foundation, then adjust examples and lesson order to the learner’s goals.


### Grammar sequence

Teach grammar patterns in the order learners need them to communicate, not as a memorized list of twelve tenses. The numbered items remain a reference list; the smaller headings show the learning phases.

#### Foundations and everyday interaction

1. **Sentence basics + *be*** — subject + verb + complement; subject/object and possessive pronouns; affirmative, negative, yes/no questions, and basic *what/who/where* questions (*I’m… / I’m not… / Are you…? / Where are you?*). Introduce *a/an/the* with concrete singular/plural nouns, then recycle them in descriptions.
2. **Present simple** — routines and facts; common verbs; *do/does* for questions and negatives; third-person *-s*. Teach *what/where/when/why/how* questions with *be* and action verbs, keeping the word order visible. Use familiar daily, study, and work examples.
3. **Ability, permission, and everyday requests** — teach *can* for present ability and common permission/request uses; *could* for polite requests and possibility. Contrast *I can check the logs*, *Can I use this?*, *Can you check this?* (direct/casual), and *Could you check this?* (polite and common). A request with *could* is usually polite, not past-time. Teach natural replies (*Sure, I can do that / Sorry, I can’t right now*). Revisit *could* for past general ability after past simple; teach *be able to* for tenses where *can* is unavailable and for specific successful outcomes (*We were able to restore the service / We’ll be able to deploy tomorrow*). For requests, add *Would you be able to…?* (softer; asks about availability) and *Would you mind + -ing…?* (polite) when learners need more softened language. Do not use *Could you be able to…?*. *Be able to* expresses ability, not permission.
#### Talking about time and experience

4. **Present continuous** — actions happening now or temporary situations; contrast directly with present simple (*I monitor alerts every day / I’m checking an alert now*). Add basic comparisons (*faster than, more useful than*) when describing a situation or choosing between options.
5. **Past simple** — completed events and experiences; regular and high-frequency irregular verbs; questions and negatives with *did*. Use personal stories as well as work updates. Introduce irregular verbs in small, useful sets with base form and past form; add past participles when learners reach present perfect. Here, revisit *could* for past general ability; for a specific completed success, prefer *was/were able to* or *managed to* (*We were able to restore the service*), while *couldn’t* is natural for a specific failure.
6. **Future meaning** — *be going to* for intentions and plans, *will* for decisions or predictions, and present continuous for arrangements. Teach common uses through examples before comparing them; add *will be able to* for future ability.
7. **Time clauses: *when, while, before, after, as soon as*** — teach these after learners can make basic present and past sentences. Use *when* for the time an event happens, *while* for actions or situations overlapping in time, *before/after* to order events, and *as soon as* for the first moment one event happens after another. Practice present, past, and future contexts (*When I get home, I’ll call you*; *While I was checking the logs, the alert fired*; *We’ll start as soon as everyone arrives*). In future time clauses after *when, while, before, after,* and *as soon as*, use a present form in the time clause, not *will* (*as soon as everyone arrives*, not *as soon as everyone will arrive*).
#### Connecting events and ideas

8. **Present perfect** — experience and a past action connected to now; introduce after past simple so learners can compare the meanings in context.
9. **Duration with *since* and *for*** — teach temporal *since* + a starting point and *for* + a duration, especially with present perfect (*I’ve worked here since 2023 / for three years*). Contrast the starting point with the length of time; introduce *since* meaning “because” later only if useful.
10. **Passive voice** — introduce after learners can form active sentences and understand basic present and past forms. Use it for processes and status when the action or result matters more than the person doing it (*The service is monitored*; *The deployment was rolled back*); then add useful negative forms (*The service isn’t monitored overnight*). Teach it in context instead of as a tense chart.
#### Choices and sentence patterns

11. **Conditions and comparisons** — introduce basic comparatives early, then extend to superlatives and *as…as / not as…as / rather than*. Teach useful *if* conditions, from real possibilities (*If the tests pass, we’ll deploy*) to simple alternatives (*If not, we’ll wait*). Teach *else* as “otherwise” in *If not, we’ll wait; otherwise, we’ll risk the release*, and in common phrases such as *someone else*; explain *or else* in context because it can sound like a warning.
12. **Indirect questions and choices** — distinguish *if* meaning “whether” in an indirect yes/no question (*I’m not sure if it’s ready*) from *whether* when presenting a choice or uncertainty (*We need to decide whether to deploy now or wait*). Show indirect-question word order (*Do you know whether it’s ready?*), and note that *whether* often sounds more formal while *if* is common in conversation.
13. **Paired (correlative) conjunctions** — teach *both…and*, *either…or*, *neither…nor*, and *not only…but also* as pairs that connect equivalent words or phrases. Keep the connected parts parallel (*We need either more time or a smaller release*). Explain emphasis and the formal tone *not only…but also* can add, plus subject–verb agreement when the paired subjects differ (*Either the engineer or the developers are available*).
#### Additional patterns to introduce as needed

14. **Other high-value sentence patterns** — add these gradually after basic sentence building and common questions:
   - **Countability and quantity:** count/non-count nouns; *some/any, much/many, a lot of, enough*; use food, shopping, and work resources to make the choices meaningful.
   - **Verb patterns:** common *verb + to-infinitive* (*want to go, need to check, decide to wait*) and *verb + -ing* (*enjoy reading, finish testing, avoid restarting*); teach frequent patterns as collocations and contrast only when useful.
   - **Hypothetical situations:** after real *if* conditions, introduce *if + past, would + base verb* for imagined choices or advice (*If I had more time, I’d practice speaking*).
   - **Reported information:** introduce basic reported statements/questions when learners need to relay what a teammate, customer, or source said; prioritize practical reporting verbs and clear time/reference changes.
   - **Adjective order:** when several adjectives describe one noun, use opinion → size → age → shape → color → origin → material → purpose/type → noun (*a beautiful small old round red Italian wooden dining table*). Add a natural shorter example first (*a nice big old brown leather bag*). Explain that speakers usually use only the adjectives they need; the order is a guide to natural phrasing, not a requirement to stack every category. “Origin” means where something comes from; the final purpose/type slot describes what it is for (*dining table, running shoes*), not a second origin category.
   - **Prepositions by context:** time *at/on/in*; place and position *at/in/on/inside/outside/under/over/next to/between/in front of/behind*; movement and direction *to/from/into/out of/through/across/along*. Teach common combinations in useful scenes (*at 3 p.m.; on Tuesday; in October; at the station; on the bus; in a car; inside the building*) and explain that Vietnamese equivalents do not map one-to-one. Start with time prepositions in Stage 2, location in Stage 3, then movement and less common relations as situations call for them.
   - **Phrasal and multi-word verbs:** teach high-frequency combinations in context rather than as a long particle list: *take off / take care of / take part in*; *turn on/off / turn up/down / turn into*; *get up / get to / get on/off / get over*; *set up / set off / set aside*; *put on / put off / put away / put up with*. Show meaning, a natural example, register, and whether the object can separate the verb and particle (*turn it off; put your coat on / put it on*) or must follow the full expression (*take care of it; take part in it; get over it; put up with it*). Recycle these across home, travel, work, and DevOps scenes; teach only the meanings needed for the current context.
   - **Comparison and choice:** *as…as*, *not as…as*, *rather than*, *whether…or*.
   - **Reason and result:** *because*, *so*, *that’s why*, and then *therefore* in more formal writing.
   - **Contrast and concession:** *but*, *although/even though/though*, *despite/in spite of*, and *however*. Contrast the forms they take: *although/even though/though + subject + verb* (*Although the test passed, the service was still slow*); *despite/in spite of + noun or -ing* (*Despite passing the test, the service was still slow*). Also teach *despite/in spite of the fact that + clause*. Explain that *even though* adds emphasis, *though* is common in conversation and can appear at the end (*It was useful, though*), and *despite of* is incorrect. Avoid combining *although* and *but* in the same clause; show punctuation and sentence position.
   - **Purpose:** *to / in order to* and *so that*.
   - **Conditions and exceptions:** *unless* after learners understand *if*; connect it to practical plans and troubleshooting.
   - **Extra information:** relative clauses with *who*, *which*, and *that* to identify people, tools, services, and incidents.
   - **Polite requests and suggestions:** *could you…?*, *would you mind…?*, *you could…*, *you should…*, and *Why don’t we…?*; show how directness and context affect tone.
   - **Modal verbs by function:** *might/may* for possibility, *should* for advice or expectation, *must* for a strong requirement or a confident conclusion, and *may* for formal permission. Contrast *mustn’t* (prohibited) with *don’t have to* (not necessary). Teach the function from context instead of presenting modals as interchangeable translations.
   - **Past habits and experience:** *used to* for past routines and *have you ever…?* with present perfect, once the underlying verb forms are familiar.

Teach negative statements as a core skill from the first lessons, not as a separate advanced topic. Revisit them with *be*, *do/does*, *did*, and later modal verbs. WH questions are also an early priority: first practice the question word plus *be*, then *do/does* and *did*, and later questions with modals and passive forms. Add other forms, such as past continuous, when they help learners tell a story, explain an incident, or describe a process. Avoid making tense names or a twelve-tense chart the starting point.

### Conversation skills and useful expressions

Teach common adverbials as reusable chunks in real sentences: time (*usually, recently, already, yet, soon*), frequency (*often, hardly ever*), manner (*carefully, quickly*), degree (*quite, very, a little*), and viewpoint or connection (*actually, probably, for example, by the way*). Explain where they usually go in a sentence and how they change tone or meaning.

Include natural ways to thank and apologize, with responses and tone differences: *Thanks for… / I really appreciate it / You’re welcome*; *Sorry about… / I’m sorry I… / I apologize for… / No worries*. Teach learners to extend a conversation through follow-up questions, reactions, and invitations to continue (*How about you? / What happened next? / That makes sense. / Could you tell me a bit more?*). Add turn-taking and repair phrases (*Can I add something? / Do you mean…? / Sorry to interrupt, but…*), and polite agreement/disagreement (*I agree because… / I see your point, but…*). Practice these in everyday, travel, study, and work exchanges, including polite chat and meeting language.

Teach learners to express desire, necessity, and obligation early because these come up in everyday plans and work conversations:

| Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *want (to)* | A preference, desire, or goal. | *I want to improve my English.* |
| *need (to)* | Something necessary to reach a goal or solve a problem. | *We need to check the logs.* |
| *have to* | An obligation caused by a rule, schedule, or outside circumstance. | *I have to join the deployment review at 3.* |

Show noun and verb patterns (*I need a break / I need to rest; I want a new laptop / I want to upgrade it*). Teach questions and negatives in useful contexts. Explicitly distinguish *don’t have to* (“it isn’t necessary”) from *mustn’t* (“it isn’t allowed”); introduce *should* for advice and *must* for strong requirements when learners are ready.

Teach modals by communicative function and common use:

| Modal | Common use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *might* | Possibility; often sounds tentative. | *The delay might be caused by DNS.* |
| *may* | Possibility, often a little more formal; also formal permission. | *The deploy may take a few minutes.* / *May I leave early?* |
| *should* | Advice/recommendation, or an expected result. | *You should check the backup first.* / *The tests should finish soon.* |
| *must* | Strong rule/necessity, or a confident conclusion from evidence. | *We must rotate this key today.* / *Several services are down; it must be a network issue.* |

Treat “might is less certain than may” as a useful tone tendency, not a precise probability scale; both can express possibility. Teach the shared pattern **modal + base verb** (*might fail, should check, must leave*), without *to* or third-person *-s*. Clarify that *mustn’t* means “not allowed,” while *don’t have to* means “not required.” For permission, compare casual *Can I…?*, polite *Could I…?*, and more formal *May I…?*.

### Vocabulary plan

- Teach **useful chunks and collocations**, not just isolated translated words: *make a decision*, *take a break*, *check the logs*, *I’m looking for…*, *Could you help me with…?*.
- Organize vocabulary by both **topic** (people, routines, food, travel, study, work/DevOps) and **communicative function** (ask, explain, compare, apologize, clarify, recommend). Reuse the same words across topics and grammar stages.
- Select early words and phrases for broad usefulness, spoken frequency, and CEFR level; add specialized terms when a topic requires them. Teach common word forms and combinations together where useful.
- For each item, show a natural Vietnamese meaning, pronunciation support, register/context, and one short realistic example. Call out common confusions instead of overloading every card with definitions.
- Keep new vocabulary sets small enough to use in a short conversation. Review older items with spaced recall and varied examples; let learners mark items as easy, unsure, or difficult so review time follows need.
- Add a **dates and time reference** for weekday names, months, ordinal dates, years, clock times, minutes, and seconds. Teach natural spoken forms as well as common written formats (for example, *May fourth / the fourth of May* and *quarter past three / 3:15*); flag ambiguous numeric date formats and teach saying the month to avoid confusion. Include pronunciation and short examples in schedules, plans, travel, and work contexts.
- Build an **irregular verbs reference and practice set** around frequent verbs, not an exhaustive list to memorize at once. Show base form, past simple, past participle, meaning, pronunciation where forms change, and one natural example. Stage past forms with past simple, then introduce participles with present perfect; practice recall in short sentences and dialogue.
- Teach **prepositions in scenes and phrases**, with time, place/position, and movement grouped separately. Start with high-frequency *at/on/in*, then common spatial and direction phrases; include collocations such as *at home*, *on the bus*, and *in a car* and use contrastive examples for common learner confusions.
- Teach **multi-word verbs in topic-based sets**, including particle meaning, common collocations, object placement/separability, and natural examples; avoid treating each verb as if it had one translation.

### Pronunciation and listening

Add a speaker control beside content where hearing the exact pronunciation helps: target words and phrases, dates and time readings, irregular-verb forms, and example sentences or dialogue turns. Each control should play only its associated item, have an accessible name and keyboard operation, and allow replay/stop. Prefer bundled recordings when a consistent model voice is needed; browser speech is an optional static-site fallback and may vary by device. Keep the written form and text pronunciation available if playback is unsupported.

Teach pronunciation as a progression, not just a speaker button: core sound contrasts and syllables; word stress; sentence stress and intonation for questions, politeness, and emphasis; then common linking and reductions in connected speech. Listening practice should move from gist to key details, with optional replay and a transcript revealed after the attempt. Decide and label a primary reference accent before producing recordings, while accepting other common accents where they do not affect understanding.

### Flexible practice

- Let learners choose a short session length (for example, 5, 10, or 20 minutes) and a goal: grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation/listening, speaking, reading, writing, or mixed review.
- Offer several practice modes: recognize meaning, recall a phrase, choose or repair a sentence, build a sentence from a cue, fill a dialogue turn, listen and identify, respond to a role-play prompt, or write a short message.
- Move from supported to open practice: model → guided choice → recall without hints → personal response or role-play. Accept more than one natural answer when the prompt allows it, and explain why an answer works.
- Adapt the next question to recent performance and learner confidence: revisit missed or uncertain items sooner, vary the context, and reduce hints as recall improves. Let learners retry, reveal a hint, skip, or choose a different activity without losing progress.
- For open speaking/writing prompts, do not imply automatic correctness scoring without a language model/service. Offer an example response, a short self-check rubric (meaning, target structure, clarity), optional local recording only when the learner chooses it, and a confidence rating; use objective answer keys for activities that can be reliably auto-checked.
- End a session with a small usable outcome (a short dialogue, voice response, explanation, or message) and a suggested next step. Keep streaks and scores optional; prioritize retrieval, feedback, and confidence over speed.

### Static hosting and learner data

The English path must work as a static site on GitHub Pages without a backend or database. Bundle lesson content and practice items with the application. Use browser-side logic for exercises, answer feedback, session selection, review scheduling, and progress suggestions. Persist learner preferences, completed lessons, and vocabulary review state in versioned `localStorage` data on the current browser/device. Explain that this data does not sync across devices and may be removed when browser data is cleared; do not imply account-based backup or server persistence. Keep the learning path usable when storage is unavailable, and provide a way to reset local progress. Browser speech may be offered as an optional enhancement, with text alternatives and a clear fallback when unsupported.

### Occasional IELTS-style practice

Use IELTS-style tasks as occasional practice for useful communication skills: organize an answer, compare options, describe a change over time, explain a process, and support an opinion with a reason or example. Prefer role-play, discussions, short presentations, and practical writing over exam sections, timed drills, or band-score training.

### First release boundary

Start with a navigable English hub and the first two groups: **Sentence foundations** and **Introductions and everyday conversation**. Include pronunciation support and simple listening activities from the start, using bundled audio or optional browser speech with text/transcript alternatives; do not require an external audio service. Use examples from personal life, travel, and work; introduce technical scenarios gradually. Add further groups after the first lessons establish the content format.

Use the **English Learning Studio** concept as the visual direction for the English path. The hub combines a learning dashboard (resume lesson, stage progress, and weekly goal), a scenario-based learning path, and a flexible practice studio where learners choose a short session and skill. Lessons open with a useful conversation or task, then explain grammar and vocabulary in context. Keep this interaction model visually distinct from Nihongo’s lesson-card/reference layout. ### Shared lesson conventions

- Show English, a natural Vietnamese meaning, and pronunciation support for new phrases. Use IPA selectively for sounds that are difficult to explain clearly in Vietnamese, and include an accessible plain-language cue; do not respell every English word as Vietnamese phonetics.
- Identify the model accent used for audio or pronunciation examples, while accepting common accent differences where they do not affect understanding.
- Keep examples natural across everyday life, travel, study, and work; include DevOps scenarios without unexplained jargon. Distinguish a direct translation from a natural Vietnamese equivalent when their wording differs.
- Provide practice that asks learners to retrieve or make a choice, not only reread explanations. Give a short explanation with feedback after an answer.
- Keep lesson pages usable on small screens and link related lessons in a clear next-step path.
